What the room is actually looking at

Thirteen numbers, one line of latitude, and a market that stays shut until somebody quotes. This is the dataset students get, what each field means, and what to put in front of them.

The dataset

Every team gets the same thing, in three forms: a labelled panel on the DATA tab, a one-click CSV, and the same numbers preloaded into the Python sandbox on the DELTAGPT tab as a dict called DATA. There is no other data. There is no telescope archive, no orbit to fit, no image to look at.

The shape of the problem

The nominal point sits 0.88° south of the line here. The ellipse is 2349 km long and tilted onto a corridor running west-southwest, so most of the uncertainty is along the track — and latitude is a curved function of distance along a great circle.

The trap, and the reason the week is called what it is. A team that takes the mean and standard deviation of latitude and reaches for Φ((line − µ)/σ) gets a confident wrong answer — typically 2 to 12 points of price on these geometries. A great circle climbs, flattens and falls, so a Gaussian spread along the ground does not stay Gaussian in latitude. The fix is to draw samples and walk each one down the corridor. It is about fifteen lines.

How real is this?

Worth being straight with the room, because a sharp student will ask.

Real, and checkable

The object, the date and time to the second, where it actually came down, its speed, size and energy, and how much warning there was. Settlement is not a simulation — the market pays out on which side of the line the rock genuinely landed. For 2008 TC3 the corridor direction is a published trajectory solution too.

Modelled, and not real

The uncertainty. Nobody publishes a covariance matrix for a four-metre rock found hours before it arrives, so the shape is taken from what impact corridors look like — long, thin, tilted — and the scale is solved so the opening question is as hard as you asked for. Students are not being handed the file JPL was working from.

How good were the real predictions?

Far better than this game, and far worse — it depends entirely on how many observations the object gave up before it arrived.

EventWarningWhat the prediction actually was
2008 TC3~19 h About 900 astrometric observations, from observers spread far enough apart to give real parallax. The final impact-point uncertainty was a 3σ ellipse of 1.4 km × 0.15 km. Meteorites were recovered along the predicted line.
2018 LA8.5 h Barely any astrometry. The early solution put the impact somewhere between the central Pacific and Africa with about 82% confidence — a corridor most of a hemisphere long. It was pinned down afterwards, from fireball video.

So the game sits deliberately in between, and does not correspond to one real published moment. A 2349 km σ is far vaguer than 2008 TC3's final answer and far tighter than 2018 LA's first one. It represents the regime a few hours in with a thin dataset — which is real — but the specific ellipse is ours, not theirs.

The honest version for students: for a well-observed impactor there would be no market at all, because the answer was known to within a kilometre eleven hours out. The uncertainty here is the uncertainty of the first few hours, held still long enough to trade.

What is genuinely the same

The thing the week is actually teaching is untouched by any of that. A real impact prediction is published as a nominal point plus a covariance in along/cross-track kilometres, turning that into the probability of landing on one side of a boundary does require sampling rather than a normal approximation, and the reason is that latitude is curved in distance along a great circle. That part is the working astronomer's actual problem, at the actual scale.

What to tell them

Six briefs, in the order they become useful. The first three are for the research window; the rest are for the bell.

Before anything else

“Download the CSV. Open DeltaGPT. Tell it what you think the answer is before you ask it anything — it won't give you a price until you do, and that's on purpose.”

The core task

“Turn that covariance into a probability. Draw at least 100,000 samples, walk each one down the corridor from the nominal point, read off its latitude, count how many land north. That fraction is what NORTH is worth.”

Every team should reach the same number to within a few tenths of a point. If two teams differ by five, one of them has a bug — usually the local basis or a diagonal covariance.

The one that separates them

“Now compute it the lazy way as well — mean and standard deviation of the latitudes, then a normal CDF. Write both numbers down. Which is right, and how much money is the difference worth at 100 shares?”

At the bell

“The books open empty. Nothing happens until you quote. Decide three things and say them out loud to your team: where you're bid and offered, how wide, and how many shares.”

The desk only takes. It will run into whoever is showing the best price, so a wide quote never trades and a tight one gets picked off when the data moves.

The free lunch to find

“NORTH and SOUTH should add to 100. If you can buy both sides for less than that, you've been handed $100 for less than $100 — and the margin knows the pair is riskless, so it barely costs you anything to hold.”

When someone asks if it's real

“The asteroid is real and the payout is a historical fact. The error bars are ours. Ask me afterwards how close the real predictions were — one of these was nailed to a tenth of a kilometre, and one of them could only be placed somewhere between the Pacific and Africa.”

On every release

“Re-run it. The ellipse always shrinks, but the nominal point moves, and it can move either way. A new solution is a new fit, not the old one with the error rubbed off. Don't assume the update points the same way as the last one.”

Things worth saying out loud

“The asteroid is real. It was really spotted on the way in, it really hit, and the market settles on where it actually came down — not on a simulation.”

“You are not being asked to determine an orbit. You are being asked to turn an uncertainty into a price.”

“DeltaGPT will write the code and argue about method all day. It will not tell you where to quote. That call is yours and it is the only part being graded.”

Operating it

Control room at /week4/admin. Build a round, open research, then open the books. The release button hands out the next solution when you want the market to move; six are available and the question gets easier each time. The noise desk has four slots — market, direction, size, frequency — and all four take liquidity and never quote, which is what makes quoting worth doing.

The panel tells you before the bell whether the round it built actually teaches the lesson: it shows, per release, how many points the normal approximation is wrong by. Under 2 and the Monte Carlo barely pays; rebuild.
